Subject: Using CiteAudit in an open citation-verification evaluation -- licence question


Dear Dr Yuan and colleagues,

I am the editor of a small mathematics education journal, and I have
spent the past several months building a free, open citation validator
after Nature reported that AI-fabricated references may already
contaminate a substantial share of the 2025 literature. The tool checks
each reference against CrossRef, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and arXiv,
and it is built to distinguish a citation it cannot verify from one it
believes is fabricated -- a conflation I found recurring in my own early
code, in general-purpose language models, and in the commercial tool
Nature relied on.

CiteAudit has been the most valuable external check I have. Because your
labels are human-assigned and your real-world group is independently
sourced, it is the only dataset I have that can tell me whether my
false-positive rate holds up outside my own test construction. It is
also, frankly, the benchmark that told me my author matching was wrong:
hand-checking the citations where we disagreed turned up several defects
in my code.

Two things I would like to ask.

First, the repository has no LICENSE file, so I have no explicit
permission to redistribute the data. My repository therefore does not
include it -- an importer downloads data/benchmark.json from your
repository on demand, records its SHA-256, and rebuilds the derived
BibTeX locally. If you are willing to attach a licence, or simply to say
that redistribution with attribution is acceptable, I would gladly ship
a pinned snapshot so that readers can reproduce the evaluation from a
single clone. If you would rather it stayed fetch-only, that is entirely
fine and nothing changes.

Second, a small finding you may want regardless. Four entries in the
real-world group look worth a second look to me:

  - 10.1109/cvpr.2017.712, cited as SphereFace, is labelled real, but
    the DOI resolves to "Binary Coding for Partial..." -- a different
    paper. The reference is real; the identifier attached to it is not.

  - citeaudit_rw_2272, the SSDD dataset paper, is labelled real and
    carries a correct DOI and title, but 12 of its 16 authors are
    misnamed (Jianwei -> Jia, Israr -> Imran, Chang -> Chao, Dece ->
    Dong, Shunjun -> Shaoyi, and others).

  - citeaudit_rw_2015, the IEDB 2018 update, is labelled real with three
    given names altered (Swapnil -> Shuchismita, Sheridan -> Silvia,
    Daniel K. -> Derin K.).

  - 10.1016/j.artint.2019.103216 is labelled real and is dated 1902 in
    the citation text for a 2020 article.

These may simply reflect that your labels answer "is this a real piece
of work?" while I was asking "does the citation match the record its DOI
points to" -- two different questions, and yours is much the harder one
to annotate. I mention them only in case they are useful.

One further observation from working with the set, which surprised me:
of the 3,356 real-world citations, only 85 carry a DOI at all. That
2.5% figure has become a central point in my own write-up, since it puts
a hard ceiling on what any identifier-based checker can reach.

The tool and all experiment logs are public, and I would be happy to
share the per-citation comparison against your labels if it is of any
use to you.

With thanks for making the benchmark available at all,

Michael Todd Edwards
Editor, Ohio Journal of School Mathematics
